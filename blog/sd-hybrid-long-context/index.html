Speculative decoding loses its long-context edge on hybrid models

On Qwen3.5-4B, DFlash goes from 1.64x on short prompts to 1.2x beyond 32K tokens. The draft is not the main problem. The target is too cheap to hide the cost of speculating.

At a glance

Decode speedup over plain decoding, batch 1, one H100, SGLang 0.5.14, from short prompts to 128K tokens of context.

Dense targetQwen3-4B + DFlash draft
2.04x→1.83x

Short → 128K. The target step grows from 4.2 to 10.5 ms and absorbs the round's fixed cost.

Hybrid targetQwen3.5-4B (24 Gated DeltaNet + 8 softmax layers) + z-lab DFlash
1.64x→1.17x

Short → 128K. The target step only grows from 4.1 to 5.5 ms, so the fixed cost stays exposed.

01

Findings

Linear attention makes the target's long-context steps cheap. That same cheapness is what takes the gain out of speculative decoding.

  1. On Qwen3.5-4B + z-lab's DFlash draft, the speedup over plain decoding is 1.64x on short prompts, 1.21x at 32K and 1.17x at 128K. We found no published long-context numbers for this pairing; the model cards and benchmarks we found use short prompts.
  2. The main cause is not a worse draft. The hybrid target costs 4.1 ms per step on short prompts and 5.5 ms at 128K, so the fixed cost of a speculative round (draft forward, draft sampling, a 16-token verify) is never amortised. About two thirds of the gap to the dense model comes from this.
  3. tau falls with context (2.97 to about 2.0), and the draft's attention window is not the reason: cutting the draft off from everything beyond 4K changes tau by at most 7%.
  4. Fine-tuning the draft on the target's own long-context outputs raises tau by 4-5% at 32K-128K. Every other change we tried was worth a few percent or nothing.
  5. If you benchmark near 128K with YaRN x4 in SGLang, keep prompt plus output under 131,072 tokens. Past that, decoding silently produces garbage and tau appears to collapse.
02

Setup

Hybrid target
Qwen3.5-4B: 24 Gated DeltaNet and 8 softmax attention layers. Draft: z-lab/Qwen3.5-4B-DFlash, 6 layers (5 with a 4,096-token sliding window, 1 with full attention), block size 16.
Dense target
Qwen3-4B, YaRN x4 beyond 32K. Draft trained by us: 5 full-attention layers, block size 16, conditioned on target layers 1, 9, 17, 25 and 33.
Dense draft data
Stage 1: the DFlash recipe's ~800K prompts (Nemotron Post-Training V2 chat, code, math and STEM, plus CodeAlpaca-20k), responses regenerated by Qwen3-4B, up to 3,072 tokens, 6 epochs. Stage 2: 26.7K prompts of 4K-30K tokens (LongAlpaca-12k, GovReport train, arXiv-summarization train), responses regenerated by the target, up to 32,768 tokens, 2 epochs.
Prompts
Held-out ShareGPT (short), GovReport test (8K), PG-19 test (32K and longer): a document cut to the target length, then a summarisation instruction. No overlap with training data.
Serving
SGLang 0.5.14, DFlash worker (DFlashWorkerV2), one H100, greedy decoding, 256 new tokens per request. Outputs with and without speculation are identical, so speed is the only thing that changes.

04

Why the speedup shrinks

Speedup is tau times the share of the round that a plain step would have cost anyway.

speedup = tau × (target step ÷ round)

At 128K the dense model gets 2.33 × 10.54 / 13.42 = 1.83x. The hybrid gets 1.94 × 5.52 / 9.15 = 1.17x. Give the hybrid the dense draft's tau and it still reaches only 2.33 × 0.60 = 1.41x.

At 32K the split is similar: 1.76x to 1.38x from the share of the round, then to 1.21x from tau. The two drafts were trained on different data, so the tau part of the gap is partly a draft-quality gap. The share-of-round part is not. It comes from the target.

Where the hybrid's round goes

CUDA-event GPU time for each stage of a round. A 16-token verify costs 5.49 ms against 4.06 ms for a 1-token decode step, and the draft side adds another 1.4 ms. Host work is 1.7-2.0 ms per round but overlaps the GPU; real GPU idle is about 0.6 ms per round. A torch-profiler trace with CPU activities suggested 1.3 ms of idle, which turned out to be profiler overhead. There is no large chunk of waste left: the round is mostly the two forward passes.

Batching and memory

At 32K the hybrid gains 1.11x at batch 8 and loses (0.95x) at batch 16, as verifying 16 tokens for every request becomes compute-bound. To commit the right recurrent state after verification, SGLang's verify kernel stores one fp32 Gated DeltaNet state per draft token per layer. With the draft loaded the server reserves 12.75 GB for these states, and the KV pool shrinks from 1.75M to 0.74M tokens. Verify kernels that avoid the stores are an active topic (TreeWY, SpecLA). In our microbenchmark they make no latency difference at batch 1 and save 3-4% of a long-context round at batch 8-16.

05

Where does tau go?

tau falls from 2.97 on short prompts to about 2.0 at 128K. More context for the draft does not bring it back.

The obvious suspect is the draft's sliding windows: five of its six layers see only the last 4,096 tokens. We changed the windows at inference time with the same weights. Greedy outputs stay identical, so any change in tau comes from the draft alone.

Removing every path to context beyond 4K costs 7% of tau at 32K, 3% at 64K and nothing at 128K. The draft's one full-attention layer, trained to see everything, gets little from far context. Whatever makes long-context tokens harder to draft, more visibility alone does not fix it. That also caps what a long-range memory for the draft, such as reading the target's recurrent states, could add.

What did help is data. Fine-tuning the z-lab draft for 15,000 steps on responses that Qwen3.5-4B itself generated, including long-context prompts, raised tau from 2.25 to 2.34 at 32K and from 2.03 to 2.13 at 128K, and cost 1% at short context. It is the largest single gain we measured. It suggests that at long context the draft is limited mostly by how well it matches this particular target, not by its architecture. We do not yet know what the rest of the gap is.

06

A few percent or nothing

  1. Smaller draft vocabulary

    +3% bs 1 · +6% bs 8

    Sampling the draft block over the 64K ids Qwen3.5-4B uses most (98.2% coverage; verification keeps outputs exact) instead of all 248K costs about 1% of tau. FR-Spec showed the same idea on dense models.

  2. Looped draft

    same tau · 3-10% slower

    Running the draft's layers (or its last two) twice per block with gated residual updates, fine-tuned against a same-data control. tau matched the control within 0.3%. In our setup (warm start from the trained draft, loss only on the final pass) the gates never opened. A from-scratch recipe with a loss on every pass might do better, but the extra pass costs about 12% of a round, so it would need a large tau gain to pay.

  3. Linear-attention drafts

    0.73-0.76x of DFlash

    On the dense model, replacing the draft's softmax attention with block linear attention added 1.4-1.6 ms per round and lost about 10% of tau. The draft's own long-context cost was only about 0.4 ms of the round at 32K, so there was little to save.

07

A measurement trap

With YaRN x4, SGLang's RoPE table ends at position 131,072. Decoding past it does not crash. It just produces garbage.

A model with a 32K native context and YaRN x4 gets rotary embeddings cached for 32,768 × 4 = 131,072 positions. We first benchmarked “128K” with 131,000-token prompts and 256 new tokens, so most decode steps ran past the end of the table. The dense model's tau dropped to 1.40 and its speedup to 1.10x, and a retrieval test of the target alone scored 0% at 131,072 tokens. With prompts cut to 130,500 tokens, tau is 2.33 (1.83x) and retrieval is 93%. If speculative decoding seems to collapse near 128K, check prompt length plus max_new_tokens against the RoPE table first.

Two smaller ones from the same project. SGLang's fused add-RMSNorm updates its residual argument in place, so reusing a residual tensor for an extra pass silently changes the stream; it cost us 4% of tau before we noticed. And flash-linear-attention's gated backward gave wrong gradients with Triton 3.4 to 3.7.0 on Hopper; 3.7.1 is fine.

08

If you serve a hybrid model

  1. Expect much smaller speculative-decoding gains at long context than on a dense model of the same size: about 1.2x at batch 1 here, and none at batch 16.
  2. The fixed costs of the round matter more than usual: the draft forward, sampling through a large vocabulary, and the multi-token verify. A smaller draft vocabulary is a cheap 3-6%.
  3. Fine-tune the draft on the target's own outputs at the context lengths you serve. It is the one change that moved tau by more than a few percent.
  4. Budget memory for verify-time recurrent states, or use a verify kernel that does not store them.
09

Caveats

One model size (4B), one GPU (H100), one serving stack (SGLang 0.5.14), greedy decoding, 256-token outputs, and DFlash v1 drafts. We did not test DFlash2 (no Qwen3.5-4B checkpoint was available), EAGLE-style drafts, larger Qwen3.5 models, or sampling. The dense and hybrid drafts were trained on different data. Batch 8 and 16 results come from smaller prompt sets than batch 1.
